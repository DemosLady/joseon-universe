Snow and Iron

눈과 쇠

Lyrics for this album
ONE

That morning was just a morning.

The tide was wrong, so they anchored in the harbour. Two sacks of rice and one barrel of water was all they needed. Three days' sailing to the capital.

Bae-swe put a hand inside his coat, checked once, and went ashore. It was there — wrapped in oiled cloth and wrapped again in hemp over that. Names the sea had kept for ten years.

The scholar stayed with the boat. So did the old fisherman. Bae-swe alone was enough for going ashore.

He haggled over a price and counted the load. The merchant asked twice what rice was worth. Bae-swe laughed and offered half, and the merchant did not laugh.

"Rice is scarce these days."

"Was it scarce yesterday?"

"Not yesterday."

He should have understood then.

TWO

At the far end of the dock, people gathered.

The way they gathered was not good. Nobody was laughing, the gestures were too large, and no one was standing where they belonged. Bae-swe had spent thirty years reading exactly that. Knowing when to run from the shape of a crowd was his trade.

The news came in pieces.

The north had been breached, they said.

The Jurchen had crossed the Amnok. The river had frozen, someone said afterwards, so it should have been obvious they would cross.

Three fortresses had fallen in three days.

Refugees were already coming over the passes. The man who said it claimed he had seen them himself. Walking with nothing, he said.

And there was one more thing.

THREE

The state was pardoning its criminals.

The dock went quiet for a moment.

Any man who could fight would not be asked about his past, they said. Let the exiles come, and the slaves, and the thieves. Come, and the crime is struck out.

Someone laughed. The laugh did not last.

"So now they call the men they spent a lifetime hunting."

"They call because there is nobody left to call."

Bae-swe said nothing. He was what they were calling.

That a piece of paper existed telling a man who had run for thirty years to come — he learned that for the first time that day.

FOUR

When he got back to the boat the scholar saw his face and asked first.

"What is it?"

Bae-swe repeated what he had heard, exactly. He kept it short. It did not take long to say.

The scholar was silent a while, then asked.

"What do we do with this writing?"

Bae-swe did not think long about the answer.

"This writing has waited ten years."

"What has waited ten years can wait one more day."

"But the people coming over that pass cannot wait for tonight."

The scholar said nothing.

The old fisherman said nothing either.

That was the last time he saw the two of them.

FIVE

He untied the halyard.

The white sail came down slowly. It was the sail he had kept dyed black all his life. It was the sail he had raised white in order to be seen. Today he simply folded it.

He left the boat. The north was a place you walked to.

From the dock he turned up the northern road. He had lived his whole life on water, and his feet on the ground felt strange.

He walked anyway.

There were two lines on that road: one coming south and one going north. The people coming down were carrying loads. The people going up were carrying nothing.

Those with something to carry went south. Those with nothing went north.

SIX

In a village on the third day he watched a decree being read.

An official climbed onto a table. His voice was young. People gathered, and nobody stood at the front.

"The king's command."

The descendants of traitors, exiles, slaves, thieves. Any man who can fight will not be asked about his crime. The state strikes it out.

When the reading ended nobody moved for a long moment.

Then one man walked out from the back. One of the older ones. Then two more. After that he stopped counting.

Bae-swe came out last. When he did, the official asked his name.

"I have none."

The official picked up his brush and set it down again, and simply nodded. There were a good many men without names that day.

SEVEN

In a village on the northern border there was a forge.

The blacksmith who had stood in that spot for forty years had not let the fire out in three days. The bellows breathed in a man's place.

What they carted in from the villages was piled in the yard.

He did not ask what it was. If it was iron, it would do.

Chains came from the magistrate's office. Who they had bound, he did not know. A pot that had cooked rice came from somebody's house. Iron came from the handle of some scholar's brush, and it was small, so it became an arrowhead.

There had been three of them. Two assistants and a boy who worked the bellows. All three had gone north first.

So he struck alone.

EIGHT

On the fourth day a bell came from the temple.

A bell that had rung for a hundred years. It filled a whole cart. It had not arrived broken — it had been broken to arrive. Someone had already cut it into pieces up on the mountain.

A monk came with it. He set it down and left without a word. The blacksmith did not ask.

On one piece, half of a flying apsara survived. A figure in flight, cut off at the waist.

When he went to put that piece into the hearth, the blacksmith stopped his hand for a moment.

Then he put it in.

Iron remembers. Melt it and it still remembers.
Even as a sword, there is a bell inside it.

NINE

That same winter, a stranger came to a village in Jeolla.

He said he had come from the sea. He said a woman on a mountain had sent him. That woman had said there was a woman in this village who knew letters.

Bun-i was forty-four then. She had taught children their letters since she was thirty, and past forty she had become the village teacher. It was twenty-eight years since she had found paper beneath the floorboards.

The stranger left a soaked ledger behind. Half of it eaten by salt, he said. The names of those who bought false testimony, he said.

And the next day he went north.

Bun-i read it for three days.

TEN

On the night of the third day she closed the ledger.

She had read every name. She had counted the places the salt had eaten. Twenty-three of them, and eleven were where a name had been.

And then she understood.

These names are already too late.

What happens if this goes out into the world now. The world was calling other names. What had come from the north had brought down three fortresses in three days, and people were coming down over every pass.

Who bought what ten years ago is a thing nobody asks today.

She wrapped the ledger in oiled cloth again and put it under the floorboards. The same place the paper had been, twenty-eight years before.

Someday somebody will find it again.

ELEVEN

The next morning she gathered paper and ink.

The villagers asked what a woman near fifty was going to a battlefield for.

"I am going to write."

"Write what?"

"The name of a man who is dying, on the day he dies."

Nobody asked anything more.

There were two things she knew. One was how to care for people — twenty years of delivering babies in that village, binding wounds, sitting beside the dying. The other was letters.

The north needed both. And people who had both were rare.

She thought about what to put on the first page, and in the end she just wrote the date. Beneath it she left one line empty.

The empty line is the place for someone still alive.

Snow was falling. She carried the ink inside her coat so it would not freeze, and walked.

That winter, fires went up on every pass. Not beacons. People.
