Iron and Blood

쇠와 피

Lyrics for this album
ONE

They told him to stand.

He asked where. Here, they said. In this line.

Bae-swe heard it twice — once with his ears and once with his feet. His feet tried to move first. Thirty years were soaked into them, and that was not his fault.

Thirty years at sea he had lived as a man who was not there. Not being where he was supposed to be was his trade. When the king's ship came he had already passed. When the light swung round he was already a shadow.

And today they tell him to stand. They tell him not to move.

Running is the one thing he learned in his life, and they are telling him not to use it.

So Bae-swe held his own feet down and stood.

TWO

The man beside him did not know who he was.

He did not ask where he had come from. A king's soldier, twenty-something by the look of him. His armour did not fit and the shoulder kept sliding down.

He was a king's soldier and Bae-swe was a pardoned thief. Two months ago that man would have come to arrest him. Today they were both just a line.

His hand shook. Bae-swe's hand shook too.

Two shaking hands levelling the same spear.

I will not call this brotherhood. But today I could die beside this man.

THREE

The drum sounded.

Bae-swe does not remember what came after it very well. There was noise, and pressure, and the ground was slippery underfoot, and somebody grabbed his shoulder and pushed him forward.

The line was pushed back. Two steps, three.

But it did not break.

Thinking about it afterwards, that was the strange part. He had no memory of holding. No memory of setting his weight.

It was not me who held.
It was the man beside me who held. And the man beside him.
And so I held too.

He had not known this. Thirty years alone, and there was no way he could have.

FOUR

When the sun came up the man beside him was still standing.

His armour was still sliding off the shoulder. There was blood on his face and he did not seem to know whether it was his own.

Bae-swe handed him a water flask. He drank and handed it back.

Neither of them said anything.

He still had not asked his name.

FIVE

Three days later the north fell back.

And the next morning the king came to the front.

Ceremonial drums sounded and the men formed up. The king came down off his horse and walked. He was young. Most of the men were seeing him for the first time.

"The north has fallen back. You did this. I know that."

"The pardon will be kept. I promise it in my own name."

The men bowed their heads.

And then a man walked out of the line.

SIX

"Your Majesty."

"Speak."

Bae-swe said that yesterday morning a man had died. That he had not died in the fighting but after it ended. That he was a pardoned man, beheaded for stealing salt ten years ago.

"He died with your paper inside his coat. And your seal was stamped on that paper."

"So I ask — what is that paper?"

The king answered.

"That pardon covers the battlefield. Outside the battlefield it does not cover. The law is so."

"It was not I who made that law. But I sit above it."

"The officer acted according to the law. And so I will not punish that officer."

I understand, said Bae-swe.

SEVEN

And then he said he would ask one more thing.

"When this fighting is over, what are we?"

"Today we are a line. And tomorrow?"

"The man who stands beside you and the man you pardoned walked the same ground and bled the same blood. Does that hold tomorrow morning too? Or tomorrow morning are we thieves again?"

The king did not answer.

It was not a thing that could be answered. You can spare an officer. You cannot promise tomorrow morning.

During that silence Bae-swe's hand went into his coat.

A bundle of soaked paper was there. Names the sea had kept for ten years. The names of the men who killed those people.

The only person in the world who could take it was standing two steps in front of him.

Two steps.
Two steps is all it would take.

He took his hand out. It was empty.

The king asked his name. He said he had none. Then let it stay that way, the king said.

EIGHT

Around that time something else was happening in the tents behind the line.

Bun-i was the kind of person who said it first: I cannot heal them. A man whose belly is open she cannot heal, and a man losing blood like that she cannot either.

What she did was hold a hand. Wet a cloth and hold it to a mouth.

And there was one more thing.

Putting her ear close and listening. Listening for what came out between the breaths. And if she could not hear it, asking him to say it once more.

She writes the name his mother called him.
Not the number the army gave him — the name they called him at home.

NINE

On the third day the paper ran out.

So she wrote on cloth. When the cloth ran out she wrote on wood — board scraps, arrow shafts, a broken spear handle.

On the morning of the fourth day she began writing on the arms of living men.

So that if he died, someone would look at that arm and read. You write it while he is still alive.

At first they all hated it. Bad luck, they said. Then one man held out his arm, and then two, and after that she stopped counting.

Park Deok-su. Kim Man-bok. Yi Dol-i.

A man took her hand and asked: did you write it?

I wrote it, she said.

And then he closed his eyes. He did not say thank you. There was no time for that.

But I heard it. That "ah". That one breath.

TEN

On the day the fighting ended, twenty-three men lay there and she had got as far as nineteen.

Four she could not ask. Their breath went first.

Those four stayed as empty lines.

And then there were hooves. One horse. In a hurry.

The fighting is over — what is so urgent?

The rider came down off his horse. There was a decree in his hand. It was sealed.

Nobody opened it.

Nobody had to open it to know. The fighting is over, so a criminal is a criminal again.

The men stood up. The wounded stood up too. A line formed again. Nobody said a word.

ELEVEN

And then Bae-swe's hand went into his coat again.

Bun-i saw that hand.

"Take your hand out."

Bae-swe looked at her.

"I know what is in there. I read it for three days myself."

"Did that paper save these people? It could not save them ten years ago. It cannot today either."

"That paper will not kill them. It will kill you."

Bae-swe said nothing.

"Have we learned nothing?"

"There was a king of twelve years old. His uncle took the child away. Six said no and died for it. And six did not die — they walked out. They threw away their office and went to the mountains."

"We remember the six who died. And we remember the six who walked out. Nobody has forgotten them."

Dying is not the only refusal.
Living is also a refusal. Walking out is also a refusal.

"Then what do we do with these names?"

"Give them to me."

"And what will you do?"

"Nothing. Only I will not lose them."

"Until when?"

"Until there is someone left to ask."

Bae-swe took his hand out. It was empty.

"You did well."

The seal was still whole. Still nobody had opened it.

Snow was falling. She put the ink inside her coat so it would not freeze, and walked.
