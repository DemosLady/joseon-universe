ONE

There are no windows in the state tribunal's prison.

So Yun-je counted the days with his fingernails. In the morning, when the gruel came in, he scratched a line on the wall. The gruel came once a day. On the day there were a hundred lines he almost stopped counting — and then scratched that day's line as well.

The first month he was angry. He went back over every sentence he had written, thinking where he might have written it differently so that this would not have happened. The second month he was sad, and the sadness was not for himself but for others: for those who died in the marketplace, for those taken while calling out names.

By the hundredth day it was nothing at all. Simply quiet.

Even without a window there was something he could see when he closed his eyes: the horizon at Jeju. It had no end. He looked at it for a long time.

TWO

On the morning the sentence was carried out, the dew was unusually clear.

As he was led out, Yun-je saw the grass beside the execution ground. It was green. It was spring. Even the grass at the execution ground knows spring has come, he thought.

A memory came to him: winter, four years old, his father setting a brush in his hand. His hand was so small that the brush was bigger than it. His father said, one who writes lives longer than a king. He had not known then what it meant.

Now he knew.

The king could kill him. But the king too would die one day. And if his writing lived a single day longer than the king, then he had won.

The officer asked, finally: have you anything to say.

Yun-je answered.

"You may cut off this head,
but you cannot cut the mouths of the people."

No one asked whether he had regrets. Had they asked, he would have said he had none. There was only one longing: to see the plum blossom on Jeju one more time, and that path where the petals blew in the wind.

THREE

Tal-swe turned his back on the capital and walked south.

He threw away the mask. He threw away the drum. And when he stood on the road with a bare face and empty hands, he did not know what he was. If I am not a clown, what am I.

In the first village he was given a cup of water. A woman asked: where have you come from.

"From the capital."

"They say something happened there."

He opened his mouth, and the tears came before the words. That was when Tal-swe first learned that he knew how to cry.

That evening he sat before the villagers and told them. Not as a song. He did not make it funny. He simply said how it had happened. They listened to the end.

In the next village he did the same. And in the one after that.

FOUR

In a tavern in Gyeongsang he met a farmer.

"I know that gentleman. The one who wrote."

"You know him, you say."

"That writing that came up from Jeju — I read it."

Tal-swe nodded.

"He is dead."

The farmer set down his cup. He was silent a long while, and then said:

"I remember that writing. I read it to my son as well."

In that moment Tal-swe understood.

The scholar's ink had not dried. It was still flowing — through his own mouth.

FIVE

The following year, on a mountain pass, Tal-swe met a young man.

He spoke with a Jeju accent. He was about twenty. They asked each other where they were headed, and then the young man suddenly asked:

"Do you happen to know that song? The one about the same charge written three times."

Tal-swe stopped walking.

"Where did you hear it?"

"I didn't hear it. I learned it by heart."

The young man recited it from beginning to end. Not one word wrong.

"Who taught you?"

"That gentleman read it to me the night before he boarded the boat. I had it in three hearings. And then he burned the paper."

Tal-swe sat down where he stood. The young man sat down too.

"Can you write?"

"A little."

"Then write it out for me once. I can remember things, but I can't write them."

The young man spread out paper. He wrote all that evening. When he had finished he handed it to Tal-swe.

Tal-swe put it inside his coat. He carried it there for thirty years.

SIX

Jeong-an walked back to Songni Mountain.

The same path going down and coming up, but her feet were different. What exactly was different she only understood much later.

The smell of smoke had settled into her robe. Three washings would not take it out. On the fourth she let it go. It was not the smell she let go of — it was herself.

She took up the wooden bell again. The same bell, a different sound.

Before, she had struck it to cover the anger. Now she simply struck it.

SEVEN

Spring came. The plum trees blossomed.

This time they were beautiful. Her mother's garden came to mind, but it did not hurt. It simply came to mind. In the sound of the summer rain she heard her father's voice. She heard it, and smiled. In autumn the leaves fell. Not fire — just leaves.

That year Jeong-an understood the thing she had wondered about for so long.

It was not that the anger had to be set down. It had to be burned all the way through. On the mountain, in prayer, it could not burn all the way — so she had to go down the mountain and become a torch. And when it had all burned, only ash was left.

She went down carrying a torch and came back with empty hands. That empty hands could be this light, she had not known her whole life.

One day that winter a man came up from below. A man with a walking stick. Roads were carved into his face.

Jeong-an handed him a cup of tea. Neither of them spoke. Even without words they knew: we both came out of the same fire.

Before he left, he asked.

"What is your dharma name?"

"Jeong-an."

"Stillness and peace."

"Yes."

And for the first time, Jeong-an thought that the name might be hers.

EIGHT

Years passed.

Tal-swe grew old. He could not walk without the stick, and his voice cracked. Still he went from village to village. When he told a story they fed him, so he did not go hungry.

He came into a small village in Jeolla one summer market day.

Children gathered under the zelkova tree. Tal-swe began the way he always did: a story about a palace, about a scholar, about fire, about ink. The children listened the way you listen to a frightening old tale. When it ended they scattered, laughing.

One girl stayed. She was about ten.

"Is it real?"

Tal-swe looked at her.

"What makes you think so?"

"Because in the middle of the story your voice changed."

Tal-swe looked at her a long time.

NINE

When he came to that village a third time, he knew he would not come again.

He looked for the girl. She was twelve now. He took her hand and said:

"This story — you will carry it on."

The girl did not know what he meant.

That night he called at her house and met her grandmother. He drew out the paper he had carried inside his coat for thirty years and laid it on the table.

"I can't read."

the grandmother said.

"Neither can I. That is why I am leaving it with you."

"Why leave it with someone who can't read it?"

"Whoever can read will want to read. Whoever reads will want to speak. And whoever speaks gets caught."

The grandmother wrapped the paper in cloth. She lifted one floorboard and put it underneath.

And for forty years no one took it out.

TEN

Bun-i grew up between the rice fields of Jeolla.

She had never learned to read. Because she was a girl. Her father put a plough in her hands, not a brush. But ears cannot be stopped. One story she had heard in the market as a child stayed inside her: about a palace and a scholar and fire and ink. The other children thought it was an old tale. Bun-i knew. It was real.

In the spring of her sixteenth year, cleaning her grandmother's room, she found something wrapped in cloth beneath the floorboards.

She opened it — hanji paper. There were letters on it. She could not read them, but she could feel them. The smell of ink was still in it. Forty years had passed, and it was still there.

She took it to the village teacher. He read it for a long while, and then tears came down his face.

"Is this that scholar's writing?"

"I don't know. What does it say?"

The teacher read it aloud to her. Bun-i listened to the end.

When he finished, she said:

"Teach me to read."

"A girl may not."

From that day Bun-i sat at the teacher's door every dawn. The first day nothing happened. Nor the second.

On the morning of the third day, the door opened.

ELEVEN

The day she first held a brush, her hand shook.

She ground the ink and drew a first stroke on the hanji. It came out crooked.

And still — it was a letter.

The scholar was dead, but the brush was there. The clown was gone, but the story was there. The nun had gone back to the mountain, but the ember was there.

Beneath the floorboards. And in Bun-i's hand.

How many people she passed the story to is not in any record. It is known only that a generation later, at the southern edge of the kingdom, another scholar was sent into exile for the crime of writing the truth, and the place he arrived at was the sea.

And the sea, unlike the land, erases nothing.
